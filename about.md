**Social and Political Philosophy:** 


- I am interested in justice and egalitarianism (How can we make things more equal for everyone?). I also have an interest in the drivers of social and political change, as well as in political motivation. A question that has interested me for a long time is how people, especially those who are dispossessed and marginalized, can, and have come to be, motivated to strive for political and social change in the face of calamity? How can they hold onto hope in the face of the hopeless? I am specifically interested in this question within the context of 20th-century African and Africana political movements, especially the civil rights movement and Martin Luther King Jr. (but also in general). 


**Moral Philosophy:** 


- I am interested in normative ethics (what we should and should not do toward others). Within this field, I am interested in non-consequentialist moral theories. Consequentialism is a collection of ethical theories that hold that we should only do things that produce the best consequences. You may know utilitarianism, which aims to bring the greatest pleasure/happiness to the greatest number of people. I don't like consequentialist theories; I find them missing something in how we think about morality (particularly the "preferences in conclusions out" way of thinking, and the theories of aggregation they often rely on). My interest in non-consequentialist ethics came through [watching an interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r3HuUKgH8PQ) of T.M. Scanlon discussing his book [*What we Owe to Each Other*](https://philpapers.org/rec/SCAWWO-5). In the interview, he says something along the lines that other moral theories seem to be too worried about other things, like the features of human beings requiring us to act in a certain way (Kant), or the amount of happiness or pleasure that can be gained through an action (Utilitarianism), and less about our relations to other people for the sake of other people. In the book, Scanlon constructs a moral theory called contractualism. Contractualism seeks to understand our moral obligations to others by appealing to principles that others cannot reasonably reject. This seems to be more in line with how we should think about morality, and I wish to explore that in more depth in the coming months.
